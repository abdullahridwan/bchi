The gap

Awareness materials rarely reach elder, limited-English immigrant households.

Cognitive health education in the U.S. is designed around routine contact with a doctor's office, not around the places elders actually spend their time. For Bangladeshi elders in the Bronx, that leaves two barriers stacked on top of each other: access, since many elders spend far more time in the community spaces around them than in a doctor's office; and stigma, since conditions like stroke, dementia, and other cognitive decline are often treated as a private, unspoken matter rather than a medical concern with recognizable warning signs.

South Asian adults face cardiovascular and stroke risk at higher rates and younger ages than the general U.S. population, according to American Heart Association research, making early symptom recognition especially consequential for this community.

Source: American Heart Association, scientific statements on South Asian cardiovascular risk
The program

Plain-language posters, placed where elders already gather.

Each poster uses plain, direct English, large type, and simple iconography over dense paragraphs, and is designed for a bulletin board, not a pamphlet rack. We start with three topics chosen for how actionable and time-sensitive they are.

Topic 01 · CDC / ASA

Recognizing stroke early

A simple recognition checklist so a family member can act in the first minutes, when treatment is most effective.

Topic 02 · Alzheimer's Assoc.

Early dementia signs

The Alzheimer's Association's published warning signs, distinguishing normal aging from signs worth a doctor's visit.

Topic 03 · CDC

Blood pressure & risk

CDC guidance on the link between uncontrolled blood pressure and stroke risk, and what regular monitoring looks like.

BCHI does not create or interpret medical content. Every poster reproduces existing public guidance from sources such as the CDC and the Alzheimer's Association; community review covers plain language and cultural fit, not clinical accuracy. These materials are for awareness only and do not replace medical advice — call 911 for a medical emergency.

How it happens

From design to the community bulletin board

01

Design in plain, direct English

Each poster is drafted around a single topic, sourced from CDC and Alzheimer's Association public guidance, and written at a reading level appropriate for a bulletin board glance, not a clinical handout.

02

Community review

Before printing, Bronx community members check the poster against its cited source for fidelity, along with tone, clarity, and cultural fit. We do not alter the underlying medical guidance.

03

Print and distribute

Posters go up in spaces elders pass through regularly across the Bronx: entryways, bulletin boards, and community rooms at mosques, community centers, and local businesses.

04

Follow up with the community

We check back with the spaces hosting posters on whether they prompted questions or conversations, and use that to prioritize the next topic.
